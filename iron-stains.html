Iron stains: what to test before you buy a filter

Red, brown, or black stains are a reason to test — not a reason to order a system from a photograph. Different metals, and iron bacteria, need different treatment. A laboratory number comes first.

Cites EPA Protect Your Home’s Water (26 February 2026), EPA 816-F-05-013 (May 2005), MDH Iron in Well Water (last updated 1 August 2025), MDH Iron Bacteria (last updated 7 January 2026), and MDH Manganese (last updated 17 November 2025). Retrieved 29 August 2026.

Do not skip the lab

EPA’s 2005 Home Water Testing fact sheet: if you are considering a home water treatment unit, find out what is in your water and what you might want to remove before contacting potential dealers. EPA’s private-well page maps “stained plumbing fixtures, laundry” to tests for iron, copper, and manganese — not to a single product.

What the stain color might mean (still test)

  • Yellow, red, or brown stains on dishes, laundry, and fixtures — MDH describes this as typical of iron. Iron can also give water a metallic taste, a rainbow sheen on tea or coffee, and can turn potatoes black. Source: MDH Iron in Well Water (1 August 2025).
  • Brownish-black or black stains on toilets, showers, tubs, or sinks — MDH describes this for manganese, which can also stain laundry and cause scaling. Source: MDH Manganese in Drinking Water (17 November 2025).
  • Blue-green stains — Penn State Extension maps these, with metallic taste especially in the morning, to tests for pH, corrosivity, copper, and lead (not iron). Source: Penn State Extension, Testing Your Drinking Water (updated 2 June 2025).

EPA also lists copper alongside iron and manganese for stained fixtures. Color is a clue, not a diagnosis.

Tests to order before you shop

  1. Iron — MDH: a laboratory can tell you the exact amount, which helps decide treatment. MDH’s point of reference: iron above 0.3 mg/L is usually considered objectionable; levels are usually below 10 mg/L. Yellow or red water often indicates iron, but test anyway.
  2. Hardness, pH, alkalinity, and iron bacteria — MDH says these are helpful in addition to iron. Treatment that works for one form of iron can fail for another, or when bacteria are present.
  3. Manganese — especially if infants drink the water. MDH guidance values (not federal MCLs): 100 µg/L or less if an infant drinks tap water or formula mixed with tap water; 300 µg/L if everyone is older than one year. MDH: bathing or showering is not the concern; drinking is.
  4. Copper (and lead if plumbing may contain it) — EPA chart for stained fixtures and for plumbing that contains lead.
  5. The CDC/EPA annual panel — coliform, nitrate, TDS, pH. MDH: iron may make it harder to get rid of harmful bacteria because some organisms need iron to grow. Iron bacteria themselves are not known to cause disease, but they can create conditions where other organisms grow. Test nitrate and coliform to be safe. See how to test.

Use an accredited laboratory. Minnesota: MDH private-well lab list. Pennsylvania: DEP-accredited labs. Ask the lab which bottle and hold time apply to metals vs. bacteria.

Know which form of iron you have

MDH: effective treatment depends on the form of iron, the chemistry of the water, and the well. Work with a water treatment specialist. In Minnesota, if you hire one, MDH says to confirm they are a licensed plumber or licensed water conditioning contractor via the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry license lookup.

Ferrous (“clear-water”) iron
Water comes out clear, then turns red or brown after standing. MDH: water softeners are the more common method; manufacturers report some can remove up to 10 mg/L, but 2 to 5 mg/L is a more common limit. A manganese greensand filter can be effective up to about 10 to 15 mg/L. Softeners are less effective if hardness is low and iron is high, or if the system allows contact with air (for example an air-charged galvanized pressure tank).
Ferric (“red-water”) iron
Water is red or yellow when it first comes out. MDH: iron filters (such as manganese greensand) are common up to 10–15 mg/L. Aeration or chemical oxidation (usually chlorine as calcium or sodium hypochlorite) followed by filtration are options if iron is more than 10 mg/L. Sediment filters, carbon filters, or softeners can remove small amounts but plug quickly.
Organic iron
Usually yellow or brown, may be colorless. More likely in very shallow wells or wells affected by surface water. MDH: organic iron and tannins can slow or prevent oxidation, so softeners, aeration, and iron filters may not work well. Chemical oxidation plus filtration may be an option. Distillation or reverse osmosis can remove any type of iron but can plug quickly if iron is high.

Those mg/L figures are MDH’s published ranges, not a shopping specification. Confirm current product ratings with a licensed professional and a current lab report.

If you also have slime or swampy odor

MDH: iron bacteria combine iron (or manganese) and oxygen into rust deposits, bacterial cells, and slime. Signs include swampy, oily, cucumber, or musty odors; yellow-orange-red-brown stains; rainbow oil-like sheen; sticky rusty slime. Confirm with a laboratory. Physical cleaning plus chemical treatment is often needed; MDH does not usually recommend continuous chlorine injection into the well. Details: MDH Iron Bacteria in Well Water (7 January 2026).

Health vs. laundry

MDH: iron in water does not usually present a health risk; most iron comes from food. Manganese is different — too much in drinking water can affect memory, attention, and motor skills, and infants are more sensitive. Do not treat “stains” as purely cosmetic until manganese and bacteria results are in.
